Typewriter ASCII

Art you type by hand. Every space is a keystroke.

What to type

Silhouettes and line drawings survive the trip to a typewriter. Group photos do not.

The picture is spelled out with this sentence, repeating over and over. It reads straight through, line after line, so someone can actually read your picture.

Hollow and stencil faces take far fewer keystrokes than solid ones. Shadowed and raised faces use a second, lighter character.

Spacing is kept exactly as given. Characters your machine cannot type are swapped for the nearest shape it has, and you will be told which.

Your machine

Switch off anything your machine lacks, or that you would rather not use. Fewer characters means a calmer picture.

Measure your machine

Type 40 capital M in one line. Then measure with a ruler from the left edge of the first M to the left edge of the last one — the same edge on both, not the width of the block of ink. That way the letter’s own side spacing cancels out and what you measure is pure carriage travel.

The same trick downwards, if you like: wind on 40 lines and measure from the first baseline to the last. Optional — line spacing is six to the inch on very nearly every machine ever built.

The paper

Wider means more detail and a great many more keystrokes.

Fine tuning

Lower smooths the picture down before it is turned into characters. Texture cannot survive a cell this size, and leaving it in reads as dirt.

These lines go on the red half of the ribbon. Type all the black first, then switch.

On the page

Nothing to type yet. Choose a picture, or type a word.
