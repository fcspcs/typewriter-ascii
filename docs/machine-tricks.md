# Getting more out of the machine

Things a typewriter can do that a printer cannot, and that most people never
use. All of it is documented in the Olympia manuals; none of it is obvious.

Written from the Olympia SM series, but the mechanisms are common to most
mechanical typewriters of the period. Element numbers below refer to the SM7
manual.

## Half spacing sideways — the correcting space bar

**There is no half-space key.** It is done with the space bar, and Olympia
only ever describes it as a correction tool. For art it is a drawing
instrument.

From the SM7 manual:

> Tap the space bar once then depress the space bar and **hold it in the down
> position**; type the first letter and release the space bar; repeat this
> action throughout the wrongly typed word.

Step by step:

1. Tap the space bar once and let go — one full step
2. Press the space bar and **hold it down**
3. Strike the character while it is held
4. Release
5. Repeat from 2

While the bar is held, the carriage advances **half a step**. The character
lands *between* two grid columns.

**Why it matters:** it doubles your horizontal resolution. Diagonals come out
smooth instead of stepped. Combined with backspace you can overlay two
characters half a step apart — shapes the rigid grid cannot make.

The manual notes a limit of about eight characters per line treated this way.

## Half spacing downwards — two ways, and the difference matters

**Way A — line space lever to the dot `( . )`**

> the platen may be revolved freely … it enables an accurate return to the
> original line position, **even when the paper is removed**.

The platen turns freely to any height. But the line advance is no longer on
the original grid afterwards.

**Way B — the line space plunger** ← *the one you want for overstrike work*

> By pressing the line space plunger inwards, the platen will be freed; this
> enables a new line position to be found.

The German SM3 manual is clearer about what happens on release:

> Releasing the line space plunger locks the platen again.

**The difference:** way A decouples the platen entirely. Way B **shifts the
zero point but keeps the grid** — after releasing, the line lever still
advances in clean whole lines, just offset by however much you turned.

**Recipe for double vertical resolution:** type the whole sheet, press the
plunger, turn half a line, release, then type a second pass. Combine with
horizontal half spacing and you have four times the cell grid.

**Finding the old line exactly:**

> The **white vertical lines** on the alignment guide should be regarded as
> being the **vertical centre of any letter**.

Line up on the centre of a character, not its edge.

## Margin release — typing outside the stops

Margin stops have hard limits. The release gets you past them:

> The ringing of the bell indicates that there are **eight more spaces**
> available before the end of the line … Pressure on the margin release,
> however, releases the mechanism. Similarly pressure on the margin release
> **enables typing to commence to the left of the limits set by the left hand
> margin stop**.

This is why the setup instructions sometimes say to hold the margin release
for the first few columns: the motif starts further left than the stop can go.

Better still, where possible: move the **paper**, using the paper guide, and
leave the stop where it can reach.

## The ribbon selector, including the setting nobody uses

From the German SM3 manual:

> With a two-colour ribbon, set the ribbon selector to **'blue'** for the
> upper colour or **'red'** for the lower. The **'white' (colourless)**
> setting is for cutting stencils.

Note Olympia labelled the upper half **blue** on some years and **black** on
others. Same position.

Two uses for art:

- **Red and black** give you two layers in one picture.
- **The white setting is the trick worth knowing:** the carriage moves, the
  type bars strike, and nothing appears on the paper. Rehearse a sequence or
  count out positions without using up a sheet.

## Red smears when you switch to red

Switching from black to red often leaves a **red streak above the first few
characters**. Not a fault.

The selector shifts the ribbon vertically. Right after a change the ribbon is
still settling, and the edge of the red half catches the top of the type
face. Strike two or three times on scrap and it stops.

## Underscore prints black even with red selected

The same mechanism, seen from the other end.

Red is the *lower* half of the ribbon, so the ribbon has to lift *higher* to
reach it — which also lifts the bottom edge of the ribbon. The underscore is
the character whose face sits **lowest**. In the red position it catches only
the ribbon's edge, or drops below it entirely and picks up old black residue
from the type guide.

Diagnostic: with red selected, type `MMMM____MMMM''''`.

- M and `'` red, underscore grey → it is the ribbon edge
- everything black → the ribbon is in the wrong way round

In order: check ribbon width (modern replacements are often narrower than the
original), check it sits full-width in both slots, clean the type guide.

**For art:** use the hyphen `-` for red rules. It sits at mid height and is
safely inside the ribbon in both positions. Type underscores in the black
pass.

## Touch adjuster

A lever on a toothed quadrant, **under the front cover, right of the right
ribbon spool**. The English manual names it but does not show it; only the
German SM3 manual illustrates it.

> The maximum pressure is obtained when the lever is pulled forward to the
> full extent.

Forward is harder and faster, back is softer.

**For art this is probably the most useful lever on the machine.** Harder
gives a more even impression and better repeatability when overstriking —
and when you are filling an area with hundreds of identical characters, any
variation in blackness shows up as a cloud. Softer is easier on the hand over
a thousand strikes.

Recommendation: set it harder and take breaks. A tired finger recovers; a
pale character stays pale forever.

It is a detented lever, nothing is under tension, and it is fully reversible.
Count the notch you start from before experimenting.
